# Diagnosing by stated pain

The pain the photographer names is the single most important input. Never run a generic
"best practices" scan instead of diagnosing to their actual words. If no pain was given, ask for it
before writing anything substantial — a guessed pain risks solving a problem they don't have.

## Getting hit by a bus, or a client with a stronger word: getting ghosted after inquiry

This is a **conviction problem, not a traffic problem.** People inquire on impulse — pretty photos,
a feeling — but were never convinced this photographer is *the one* worth the investment. When the
photographer follows up, there's no particular reason to pick them over the several others who
looked and sounded similar, so they go quiet.

The three fixes that reduce ghosting all raise conviction *before* the visitor hits inquire:
1. **Differentiation / supply of one** — a reason it's *this* photographer, not a generic mood or
   style-word soup every competitor also uses.
2. **Findable, sufficient proof** — trust surfaced where couples are deciding, not buried on a
   sub-page or thin/low-contrast at the bottom.
3. **A lower-commitment on-ramp + a clear "what happens after you reach out"** — so warm-but-not-
   ready couples don't send a lukewarm impulse inquiry and vanish, or leave with no way to stay in
   the photographer's world.

Do NOT default to "more inquiries" opportunities (steps sections, freebies) as the *lead* fix for
ghosting — the volume isn't the problem, the quality of conviction is. Freebies/on-ramps still
matter (#3 above) but as a *retention* mechanism for the not-yet-convinced, not as the headline fix.

## Low inquiry volume / "just general feedback" / "not enough inquiries"

More standard funnel-leak diagnosis. Look for:
- A hero that doesn't orient (no who/what/where/next-step)
- A trust gap (thin or buried proof)
- No guided path to the contact form (competing links, quiet CTAs, or a homepage that dead-ends into
  social media instead of the inquiry form)
- No freebie/lead magnet to capture the not-ready-yet visitors

## Better-fit / higher-quality inquiries (not volume — quality)

Look for what's *attracting the wrong people*: vague or overly broad positioning ("I shoot anything,
anywhere"), pricing that doesn't pre-qualify, or hero/guide copy that doesn't name the specific
dream client. The fix is sharper differentiation and clearer self-selection (multi-service landing
pages, a hero that names who it's for), not just "more CTAs."

## Wanting to raise rates

The gap is usually that **perceived value doesn't yet support the price.** Look at whether proof,
production value, and positioning match the rate the photographer wants to charge. The fix sequences
trust and differentiation *ahead of* where pricing appears, so the number feels justified by the
time a visitor reaches it. Do not tell them to hide or delay pricing — pricing on the page is a
legitimate filter for budget-mismatched leads. The fix is raising the proof around it, not hiding
the number.

## "Just general feedback" / clearly a hobbyist, not trying to book more work

This is a **non-fit signal, not a diagnosis request.** If someone says it's a hobby, they're not
worried about it being professional, or their goal is vague/absent of any business intent, do not
write a full three-opportunity roadmap or push the sales call. Give one genuine, warm compliment or
tip and a soft door ("if it ever grows into something you want to take further..."). Spending a full
roadmap and a call on a self-identified non-buyer wastes the cheap-leak-vs-expensive-leak logic the
funnel is built on.

## When Maggie hands you an initial gap list

Treat it as a starting hypothesis, not a locked answer. Before writing full copy:
- Check whether the listed gaps actually match the *mechanism* of the stated pain (e.g., "missing
  CTA" is real but minor compared to a hero that doesn't differentiate at all, for a ghosting case).
- Fold any five-minute/cosmetic fix into a larger opportunity as supporting evidence rather than
  giving it its own slot, unless Maggie explicitly wants it separated (she sometimes does — when she
  says "these are separate pieces," treat them as genuinely distinct opportunities and do not
  re-merge them).
- Say plainly if a listed "problem" isn't really a problem (e.g., showing pricing isn't premature —
  it's a legitimate filter) and reframe to what's actually happening.
- Say plainly if the site is strong enough that this should be an *elevation* roadmap (good → even
  better) rather than a *repair* one (broken → fixed). Never manufacture a gap to avoid saying "this
  is already working well."

Always surface this reasoning to Maggie before finalizing copy — she reviews the diagnosis far more
than the prose, and her corrections are final. If she corrects your framing, apply it exactly as she
states it rather than a softened or re-blended version.

## When a specific finding is handed to you (not inferred)

If Maggie gives you a literal, verifiable detail — a CTA button that isn't hyperlinked, a headline
that's actually good and shouldn't be flagged as a gap, a specific line that's off-center — use that
exact finding. Don't genericize it into a softer or more generic-sounding observation, and don't
"correct" a strength she's already identified back into a manufactured weakness.
